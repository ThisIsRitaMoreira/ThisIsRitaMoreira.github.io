# `data/retail_extra.js`: schema and methods

`window.DATA_RETAIL_EXTRA = {...}` is generated by `analysis/retail_promo_basket.py`. Do not edit it by hand.

It powers the *Retail & grocery operations* capsule, part 2: **what promotions really do**, **what sells together**, and the **promo order optimiser** (Excel Solver).

```
python -I analysis/retail_promo_basket.py <folder with products.rda, transactions.rds, promotions.rds> <folder with pyreadr> <site root>
python -I analysis/build_solver_workbook.py <site root> [<folder with openpyxl>]
```

Run them in that order. The first script writes `data/retail_extra.js` and `downloads/tableau/promo_performance.csv`. The second reads `order_plan` from the JS and writes `downloads/promo_order_optimizer.xlsx`. It then re-solves the plan and checks every workbook formula against it.

**Source:** 84.51° *The Complete Journey* (2017, 2,469 households), via the `completejourney` R package. Merchandise only: fuel, coupons, kiosks and other non-merchandise departments are excluded, as in `store_ops.py`.

**Weeks.** Weekly series cover **weeks 2–52**, i.e. 51 weeks from Mon 2 Jan to Sun 24 Dec 2017. Week 1 is a single day (1 Jan, 382 baskets against about 2,900 in a normal week). It would distort the local baselines, so it is left out of the promotion analysis. The basket analysis uses every basket in weeks 1–52. Every weekly array in this file has 51 entries that line up with `source.weeks` and `source.week_start`. (`store_ops.js` uses 52 entries for weeks 1–52; don't mix the two axes.)

**Conventions:** shares are fractions (`0.25` = 25%). Money is US$, because the data is from a US chain. "Units" are the panel's units: a 12-pack and a 2-litre bottle each count as 1.

## `promo`: what promotions really do

### Method (also in `promo.method`, ready to print)

1. **Promotion week.** The SKU is in the mailer at ≥25% of the stores that carry it (`rules.mail_threshold`), the same rule as the order recommender in `store_ops.py`.
2. **Extension.** The loyalty-card price often outlasts the mailer. The promotion continues for up to `rules.ext_max_wks` (2) more weeks while the SKU's discount stays ≥`rules.ext_pp` (5 points) deeper than its usual discount. These weeks count as promotion weeks.
3. **Baseline.** For each SKU and week: the mean units in the SKU's *ordinary* weeks (no promotion, not in the 2 weeks after one) within ±6 weeks. The current week is left out. The window widens to ±13 weeks, then the full year, if fewer than 4 ordinary weeks are available. So the baseline follows trend and season.
4. **Gross lift** = Σ (units − baseline) over the promoted SKU's promotion weeks.
5. **Post-promotion dip** = Σ (baseline − units) over the 2 weeks after each promotion (`rules.post_wks`). Positive means sales fell below baseline (pantry loading). Negative means they stayed above it.
6. **Cannibalisation.** For each category, a regression across the 51 weeks. The outcome is how far the category's *non-promoted* SKUs sit above or below their own baselines that week. The driver is that week's gross lift. The controls are store traffic (baskets that week vs the surrounding weeks) and the non-promoted SKUs' own extra discount. `cannib_rate` = −slope: units lost by sister SKUs per extra promoted unit. A negative rate (a "halo") is reported, but it is never credited: cannibalised units are 0.
7. **Net incremental units** = gross − cannibalised − dip.
8. **Scope.** SKUs on sale all year: first sale in weeks 2–7, last sale in 47–52, ≥20 selling weeks, ≥8 ordinary weeks. This drops launches, delistings and very slow lines. `coverage` gives the share of category units in scope.

`promo.limits` (array of strings) gives the caveats, ready to print: it's observational, a panel, same-category/same-week only, units mix pack sizes, supplier funding is unknown, and which categories' cannibalisation is clearly above zero.

### `promo.rules`

`mail_threshold` 0.25, `display_threshold` 0.25, `ext_pp` 0.05, `ext_max_wks` 2, `post_wks` 2, `baseline_window` 6, `min_weeks_sold` 20, `min_ordinary_wks` 8, `life_wks` 6.

### `promo.display`: did in-store display add lift?

* `overall.with_display` / `overall.mailer_only`: `{events, lift_pct, depth}`. `depth` is the median event discount.

## `basket`: what sells together

**Method** (also in `basket.method`, ready to print):

* Each basket becomes the set of product categories in it.
* **Support** is the share of all baskets containing both categories.
* **Confidence(A→B)** is the share of A's baskets that also contain B.
* **Lift** is support / (share with A × share with B), i.e. how many times more often the pair occurs than chance.
* **`lift_adj`** is the observed count divided by what independence predicts *within basket-size strata* (≤2, 3–4, 5–7, 8–11, 12–17, 18–25, 26+ categories). Pairs that only co-occur because both turn up in big weekly shops drop towards 1.
* Categories in <0.5% of baskets are dropped (189 remain). Pairs need support ≥0.5% (`min_support`).

| Key | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `n_baskets`, `min_cat_share`, `min_support` | As above |
| `categories[]` | All 189 categories, sorted by penetration: `{cat, label, dept, zone, baskets, pen}`. `pen` = share of baskets. `zone` is one of Fresh, Dairy & frozen, Drinks, Pantry, Non-food, Other |
| `pairs[]` | Top 40 pairs by lift (support ≥0.5%): `{a, b, a_label, b_label, n, support, conf_ab, conf_ba, lift, lift_adj, cross_zone}`. `conf_ab` = P(b \| a). `cross_zone` = the two sit in different store zones (the harder adjacency calls) |
| `network.nodes[]` | `{id, label, zone, pen, x, y, degree}`. `x`,`y` ∈ [0.06, 0.94] come from a fixed-seed force layout; scale them to the SVG. Size by `pen`, colour by `zone` |
| `network.edges[]` | `{s, t, lift, support}`. `s`/`t` are node `id`s. Edges have lift ≥1.5 and support ≥1% (`network.rule`), top 70 by lift. Width by lift |
| `recommendations[]` | 5 merchandising actions: `{id, head, body, pairs[]}`. `pairs` uses the same shape as `pairs[]` and is oriented so that `conf_ab` is the number quoted in the text |

## `order_plan`: the promo-week order LP (Excel Solver workbook)

One real mailer week (`week` 28, starting Mon 3 July 2017, the July 4th mailer) and 12 SKUs promoted that week. The SKUs are picked round-robin across categories, by forecast.

**Model.** Choose cases `x_i` (integer) and units sold `s_i`.

Maximise Σ margin_i·s_i − leftover_cost_i·(pack_i·x_i − s_i), subject to:

* s_i ≤ forecast_i
* s_i ≤ pack_i·x_i
* x_i ≥ min_cases_i
* Σ unit_cost_i·pack_i·x_i ≤ `budget`
* Σ ft3_case_i·x_i ≤ `space`

It is solved with scipy `milp` (HiGHS).

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `week`, `week_start` | The mailer week |
| `budget`, `space` | Order budget ($) and space (ft³). ASSUMED: see `assumptions.budget_rule` / `space_rule` (both bind at the optimum) |
| `assumptions` | `cost_pct_of_regular` 0.70 · `vendor_funding_of_discount` 0.50 · `leftover_cost_pct` {Ambient 0.05, Chilled 0.20, Frozen 0.08} · `ft3_per_unit` by category · `pack_rule` · `min_rule` · `budget_rule` · `space_rule` · `store_scale` 1.0 |
| `skus[]` | See below |
| `result` | `objective` (optimal contribution $) · `lp_relaxation` (fractional cases allowed) · `naive_objective` (rule of thumb: cut every SKU by the same %, then top up the biggest gaps while budget/space allow) · `uplift_vs_naive` · `budget_used` · `space_used` · `units_ordered` · `units_sold` · `forecast_units` · `fill_rate` · `naive_fill_rate` · `revenue` |

`skus[]` fields:

* **REAL:** `id`, `name`, `supplier`, `cat`, `section`, `zone`, `base` (the SKU's baseline that week), `lift` (units/baseline in its *other* mailer weeks), `actual` (units really sold that week; for comparison only), `reg_price`, `promo_price` (what shoppers paid that week).
* **ASSUMED:** `pack`, `min_cases`, `ft3_unit`, `leftover_pct`.
* **DERIVED**, computed exactly as in the workbook:
  * `forecast` = ROUND(base × lift × store_scale)
  * `depth` = 1 − promo/reg
  * `unit_cost` = 0.70·reg − 0.50·(reg − promo)
  * `margin` = promo − unit_cost
  * `hold` = unit_cost × leftover_pct
  * `ft3_case` = pack × ft3_unit
* **SOLUTION:** `opt_cases`, `opt_sold`, `opt_left` (optimal plan). `naive_cases`, `naive_sold` (rule of thumb; the workbook's Model sheet opens on this plan).

## The Excel workbook (`downloads/promo_order_optimizer.xlsx`)

| Sheet | What |
|---|---|
| Instructions | The question, what's real or assumed, and how to run Solver. Includes the manual set-up table in case the saved model doesn't load |
| Inputs | Global assumptions (orange) and the 12 SKUs. Real data is blue, assumptions orange. Forecast, depth, unit cost, margin, leftover cost and ft³/case are formulas |
| Model | Decision cells F5:F16 (cases) and H5:H16 (units sold), in yellow. Formulas for units ordered, leftovers, cost, space and contribution. A constraint block with OK/Check status cells. Objective in C28 |
| Answer | The Python (scipy/HiGHS) optimum, the rule-of-thumb plan and a summary (budget/space used, fill rates, LP-relaxation bound) |

**Saved Solver model.** The workbook stores it as hidden, sheet-level defined names on *Model*, the same way Excel saves one:

* `solver_opt`=Model!$C$28, `solver_typ`=1 (max)
* `solver_adj`=Model!$F$5:$F$16,Model!$H$5:$H$16
* `solver_num`=6, with `solver_lhs1..6` / `solver_rel1..6` / `solver_rhs1..6`. Relations: 1 = `<=`, 3 = `>=`, 4 = `int` with rhs `"integer"`
* `solver_eng`=2 (Simplex LP), `solver_neg`=1 (non-negative), `solver_tol`=0 (integer optimality 0%)
* Excel's other default option names

Data > Solver should open pre-filled when the Model sheet is active. If an Excel build ignores them, the Instructions sheet has the manual set-up.

`build_solver_workbook.py` checks its own output without Excel:

1. A small formula evaluator computes all 261 formulas.
2. The inputs it computes are re-solved with scipy and must give the same plan and objective as `order_plan.result`.
3. The Model's objective and every constraint check are evaluated at the optimum and at the rule-of-thumb plan.
